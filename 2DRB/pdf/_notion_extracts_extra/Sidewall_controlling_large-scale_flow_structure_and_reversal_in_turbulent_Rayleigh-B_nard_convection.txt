1. Introduction
The Rayleigh-Bénard (RB) convection, i.e. a fluid layer heated from below and cooled from
above, is one of the fundamental models conceived for the purpose of investigating the
buoyancy-driven convection in astrophysical, geophysical and engineering problems [1,2].
This system is characterised by the Rayleigh number Ra = αgH 3 /(νκ) and the Prandtl
number Pr = ν/κ, where H is the RB cell height, g is the gravitation magnitude,  is the
temperature difference between top and bottom plates, ν, κ, and α respectively are the
kinematic viscosity, the thermal diffusivity, and the thermal expansion coefficient of the
working fluid. When Ra exceeds the critical Rayleigh number, the convective instability
occurs, thermal plumes triggered from the bottom and top thermal boundary layers (BL)
self-organise into a large-scale circulation (LSC) [3–6]. One intriguing and crucial feature

of the LSC is the stochastic and subsequent reversal of its circulation orientation. The phe-
nomena of LSC reversal involved turbulent RB convection has been studied extensively in
the past two decades.
    The theoretical and experimental works on LSC reversals in turbulent RB convection
are rich. Deterministic or stochastic models [7–10] were developed to describe the LSC
dynamics and capture the physical mechanisms of LSC reversals. Assaf et al. [11] extended
the previous models and achieved to predict the tails of the probability distribution of LSC
reversals, which agree well with the experimental data. Sugiyama et al. [12] experimen-
tally analysed the LSC reversal in thermal convection in a rectangular geometry and found
that the corner vortices play an important role in the reversal. It is shown that as thermal
plumes detach from the BLs, the corner vortices grow both in size and in kinetic energy
contained, then take over the large-scale diagonal roll, thereby resulting in the flow rever-
sal. Wang et al. [13] further pointed out that the LSC reversal is stimulated by the rare
massive eruption of thermal plumes. The finding of Vasil’ev et al. [14] showed that the
LSC reversal process depends on both the Rayleigh number and the aspect-ratio between
the cell thickness and the side length in the circulation plane. Ni et al. [15] presented
an experimental study on the LSC reversal in quasi-two-dimensional (2D) turbulent RB
convection. Recently, Chen et al. [16] discovered a transition in LSC reversal frequency
in the quasi-2D geometry, which corresponds to a change in flow structure from a single
roll regime to a less-stable abnormal single-roll regime with substructures inside the main
roll. It is also shown that although the corner vortices trigger the event of LSC reversal, the
reversal frequency mainly relates to the LSC stability. In order to study the quantitative role
of the corner vortices on the reversal. the experiments on RB convection in a corner-less
cell were carried out and it is found that removing the corner significantly decreases the
reversal frequency [17].
    Since the rapid development of computational capacity, direct numerical simulation
(DNS) and analysis of RB convection and LSC dynamics become popular. In numerical
studies of the LSC reversal, (quasi) 2D rectangular geometry is usually chosen to elimi-
nate the three-dimensional dynamic feature of the LSC [18,19], such as the torsional or
sloshing effect found in cylindrical geometry [20–23]. Through Fourier decomposition
analysis on DNS data, Chandra et al. [24,25] showed that the large-scale Fourier modes
are effective tools to characterise the flow structure and reversal in RB convection and
large fluctuations in time series of heat transport efficiency are located in the process of
flow reconfiguration during the reversal. Podvin et al. [26] applied the proper orthogonal
decomposition analysis on the dynamics of LSC in a 2D RB square cell and showed that the
LSC reversal can be identified by sharp fluctuations in the amplitude of each modes. Huang
et al. [27] presented a numerical study on turbulent RB convection using the fixed heat flux
for boundary conditions and proved that the fixed heat flux boundary condition has the
ability to suppress the reversal frequency. Xia et al. [18] numerically investigated the LSC
reversal in 2D RB convection with accounting for the non-Oberbeck-Boussinesq effects.
Xu et al. [28] discussed the dependency of the reversal event on the Prandtl number in 2D
circular convection geometry. Wang et al. [29] studied the tilt influence on the LSC rever-
sal in a 2D cavity and found that a small tilt angle can lead to a drastic decrease of reversal
frequency. Dong et al. [19] discussed the roughness distribution effects on the flow struc-
ture and pointed out that the asymmetric wall roughness can promote the LSC reversal
events.

   To our knowledge, many strategies have been proposed to control turbulent RB con-
vection, such as imposing vibrational pulsation [30], applying wall roughness [31–33],
introducing wall temperature oscillation [34,35], through confinement [36,37], using
active particles [38], via reinforcement learning[39], etc. However, most of these focus on
controlling the heat transport mechanism, but the research on controlling the structure
and reversal of LSC still lacks. Recently, Zhang et al. [40] introduced the local constant-
temperature condition on sidewalls to successfully control the LSC reversal when the
sidewall constant-temperature condition is imposed in the proper location and size. In this
paper, we proposed a new control approach – imposing small solid rectangular controllers
on sidewalls to stabilise the dynamics of corner flows and eliminate the LSC reversal. This
paper is organised as follows: Section 2 details the governing equations of RB system and
numerical methods; Section 3 gives the results obtained for the sidewall controlling LSC
reversal in turbulent RB convection; the conclusions are given in Section 4.

2. Numerical set-up

small solid rectangles of width w and height h are introduced on the cavity sidewalls to
control the large-scale flow structures and reversals, as shown in Figure 1. The left rectan-
gular controller is installed at a distance of l from the cold (top) wall and the right one at
the same distance from the hot (bottom) wall.

The LSC reversal event is identified using the criteria as followed by Huang and Xia [46]:
(i) the clockwise (L(t) < 0) and counter-clockwise (L(t) > 0) LSC state should be clearly
distinguished from each other; (ii) when the LSC transits from one state to the other, L(t)
should first cross over the maximum of the current state; (iii) the LSC at new state should
keep for more than one turnover time. Note that all statistics for each run are calculated
over an averaging time of more than 4000 turnover times.

way to control the LSC reversals in turbulent RB system.

4. Conlusions
The spontaneous LSC reversals commonly take place in standard RB turbulence in a cer-
tain range of Ra and Pr and the corner flows play a crucial role in the reversal event. In
this paper, we report a novel sidewall controlling strategy to stabilise the corner flows and
eliminate the LSC reversals. Through a series of direct numerical simulations in 2D RB
turbulence at Ra = 108 and Pr = 4.3, it is shown that when the rectangular sidewall con-
trollers are installed near the top of corner vortices obtained from standard RB convection,
it is very effective to stabilise the growth of corner flows, thereby achieve to suppress the
LSC reversal. As the controllers move towards the plates (decreasing l), the corner vortices
can be stabilised and the LSC reversal successfully eliminated if the width w is large enough.
As the controllers move towards the cell centre (increasing l), it is found that they easily
break up the large-scale main roll, therefore, make the flow structures complicated; for
very large width w, the single roll can be completely broken into double rolls. In addition,
the sidewall controllers have the ability to slightly enhance or suppress the heat transport,
when the width w and the distance l are in the proper ranges.
    The findings of our works open a new avenue to control the LSC reversal through impos-
ing small controllers on the sidewalls. This kind of control strategies can be easily realised
in engineering, in which only small modifications are applied on the geometries.
