Large-Eddy Simulation (LES) of turbulent ﬂows, in which grid reﬁnement is often necessary near solid boundaries whereas

    The lattice Boltzmann (LB) method originates chronologically with the seminal work of McNamara and Zanetti [3] fol-
lowed shorty after by the article of Higuera et al. [4]. The latter really puts the LB method on the map of computational
ﬂuid dynamics. Applications to complex industrial or real-life ﬂows have been demonstrated ever since [5,6].
    Although the lattice Boltzmann (LB) method has proven its ability to simulate complex ﬂows on (suﬃciently ﬁne) uni-
form grids, improvements are still needed to tackle turbulent ﬂows on composite multi-resolution grids, especially in the
framework of LES. The main diﬃculty arises from the “lack of deformability” of the lattice. As recently quoted by Sagaut [7],
the development of LES within the framework of the lattice Boltzmann method is still at a very early stage.
    While conventional methods start with a continuous description of the ﬂuid at a macroscopic level, e.g. the Navier–Stokes
equations, the LB method considers the ﬂuid at a mesoscopic level, intermediate between the microscopic and the macro-
scopic [8]. Concretely, the ﬂuid is modeled as populations of (ﬁctitious) particles that collide, re-distribute and propagate
along the different links of a discrete lattice, so that the desired “collective dynamics” can be recovered in the continu-
ous limit [9]. The complexity of the ﬂow emerges from the repeated application of simple rules of collision and streaming
possibly supplemented by bounce-back or reconstruction procedures at the boundaries [10]. The LB method expresses the
evolution of the probability distributions of these populations on the lattice, or grid. Originally, the method was designed
for simple structured grid with a constant spacing in the three directions. Nevertheless, multi-resolution may be fulﬁlled
by embedding domains of different (spatial) resolutions within the original grid (see Fig. 1). In that situation, some speciﬁc
conditions are required to determine the mapping of the probability distributions at the boundary between two resolution
domains. For the simulation of turbulent ﬂows, this mapping is essential since it should account for the discontinuity of the
rate of strain (related to the change of spatial resolution) and suitably encompass effects related to possible subgrid-scale
turbulent dynamics.
    In Section 2, the general framework of the LB method is brieﬂy recalled and the position of the problem is presented. Our
proposal is introduced and discussed with respect to existing works in Section 3. In Section 4, the results from test compu-
tations of a turbulent channel ﬂow are reported and compared with reference data from direct and large-eddy simulations.
Conclusions and perspectives are drawn in the last section.

domain, the lattice is uniform and the computational performance of the method is thus preserved. Nevertheless, some
inconsistency in the connectivity of boundary nodes requires to correctly map the probability distributions at the transition
between two domains (Fig. 3).

    Concerning the prolongation from a coarse grid to a ﬁne grid, a common practice is to simply copy the value at coinciding
nodes and resort to interpolation at intermediate ﬁne nodes [15]. As a matter of fact, this is a low-level approximation that
does not explicitly account for the enrichment of turbulent ﬂuctuations on the ﬁne grid [29]. In a LES, this enrichment
is a priori essential since turbulent excitations are expected to populate the whole resolution bandwidth. Nonetheless, we
have disregarded this feature in our study and prolongation has been simply estimated by third-order interpolation. Test
computations for a turbulent channel ﬂow (reported in Section 4) will show that this neglect does not signiﬁcantly impact
the results.
    In principle, the prolongation step is expected to “de-smooth” the collision term and recreate some grid-scale excitations
on the ﬁne grid. This operation is delicate and prone to numerical instability, especially in the turbulent regime. Our at-
tempts to approximate the prolongation operator by inverting the previous Gaussian box-ﬁlter, i.e. P = R−1 ≃ I − (σ 2 /2),
have led to strong perturbations of the numerical solution. Finally, even if an improved prolongation procedure may be
desirable in ﬁne, we believe that “enrichment” is partially achieved in our coarse-to-ﬁne mapping by accounting explicitly
for turbulence in the relation (19) between τ g∗c (xb , t ) and τ g∗f (xb , t ).

3.4. Uniﬁed framework

   Interestingly, if one excludes the restriction operation and disregards subgrid-scale effects, our proposal eventually re-
duces to the formula introduced by Filippova and Hänel [19] and revisited later by Dupuis and Chopard [20]. As previously
argued, the domain of validity of these approaches is restricted to DNS on suﬃciently ﬁne grids (in order to validate
Eq. (10)). If one retains the restriction operation but disregards subgrid-scale effects, our proposal resembles the method
proposed by Lagrava et al. [15] by identifying the restriction with their ﬁltering procedure. The latter has proven itself
to improve the numerical stability of the LB scheme in presence of strong gradients, but does not explicitly account for
subgrid-scale effects. In this regard, it is therefore restricted to DNS as well. Our proposal may be viewed as an attempt to
conciliate these different aspects to address the LES of turbulent ﬂows.

4. Tests computations of a turbulent channel ﬂow

    A turbulent channel ﬂow has been used to test the capabilities of our multi-resolution LB scheme (Fig. 4). This academic
ﬂow conﬁguration is of importance in engineering-ﬂow problems and is commonly used as a prototypical case to investigate
non-homogeneous wall-bounded turbulence [30]. It is well documented in the literature and can beneﬁt in particular from
the comprehensive database built by Moser et al. [31]. Some preliminary LB computations (DNS without subgrid-scale
modeling) on uniform grids have already been performed [32].
    Our simulations are referred to as LaBS (for Lattice Boltzmann solver) in the following. The scheme is based on the
standard D3Q19 lattice and relies on the BGK approximation. The speed of sound is arbitrary and ﬁxed to optimize the
long-time convergence of the simulation while ensuring that the Mach number remains small, typically Ma ≃ 0.15. The
no-slip condition is enforced at the boundaries by using the method developed by Verschaeve and Muller [33], which is a
generalization of the model introduced by Skordos [34] to off-lattice boundaries. The strategy of this boundary treatment
is to accurately interpolate or extrapolate macroscopic ﬂow quantities at boundary nodes, and reconstruct the probability
distributions from these quantities. In our simulation, the ﬁrst ﬂuid node is at a distance 0.5x (half-spacing) from the wall.
This reconstruction method is consistent with the overall second-order accuracy (in space) of the LB scheme. A uniform
body force is added to the dynamics in the streamwise direction to drive the ﬂow against the viscous forces. This force is
adjusted dynamically to reach the target averaged ﬂow rate across the channel, as detailed in [35]. This external body force
is included in the collision step according to the procedure established by Guo et al. [36]. Finally, the subgrid-scale viscosity
relies on a shear-improved variant of the Smagorinsky model (SISM) developed by Lévêque et al. [37]. Namely,
                                                        
       νsgs (x, t) = (Cs x)2 S(x, t ) − S(x, t )                                                                                               (22)
where Cs is the usual Smagorinsky constant, x is the local grid spacing and S(x, t ) is the resolved rate of strain. Here, the
rate of strain cannot be easily computed from the distributions. It is better evaluated (at second order) by differentiating
the velocity ﬁeld reconstructed from Eqs. (2). The correcting term to the Smagorinsky viscosity is the norm of the averaged
rate of strain. The average (indicated by the tilde) is here meant in time and is estimated by using a recursive exponential
ﬁlter, as detailed in [38].
    The SISM accommodates the effects pertaining to the presence of a strong shear by reducing signiﬁcantly the value of the
Smagorinsky viscosity in that situation, e.g. near a solid boundary. Interestingly, this model does not call for any adjustable
parameter besides Cs = 0.18 that is ﬁxed for all ﬂows [21]. There is no need for any empirical Van Driest damping function
nor any kind of dynamic adjustment of the Smagorinsky constant [2]. The simplicity and computational manageability of
the original Smagorinsky model are therefore preserved. The “shear-improved” correction is based on physical arguments
originally introduced by Toschi et al. [39]. The SISM has already been validated in various complex ﬂow conﬁgurations,
and has proved itself to be a relevant alternative in order to address non-homogeneous (possibly unsteady) turbulent ﬂows
without adding signiﬁcant complication and computational cost to the standard Smagorinsky model [40–42].
    In the following, the grid-scale velocity components are U + u , v and w along the streamwise, wall-normal and span-
wise directions, respectively. U ( y ) is the mean velocity, whereas u (x, t ), v (x, t ) and w (x, t ) are (instantaneous)

velocities. The distance to the wall is y. The Reynolds number is deﬁned as Reτ = u τ H /ν , where u τ = τ w /ρ is the skin-

5. Conclusion

    In summary, this study addresses in a physically-sound framework the multi-resolution simulations of turbulent ﬂows by
the lattice Boltzmann method. Our approach conciliates previous works in a uniﬁed description and extends them to suitably
account for subgrid-scale dynamics in the context of LES. Importantly, the key equation (19) governing the mapping of the
lattice relaxation times (at resolution changes) is general and does not explicitly depend on the subgrid-scale viscosity
model. Also, our study relies on the BGK approximation for the collision operator, but a similar approach could a priori be
followed for any other approximation, such as the MRT approximation.
    Rohde et al. [49] have recently advocated a cell-centered formulation (rather than the standard cell-vertex description) to
address multi-resolution lattice Boltzmann simulations. In that case, the inconsistency in the connectivity of boundary nodes
is alleviated, and our restriction and prolongation operations are naturally accounted by simply redistributing (merging or
splitting) the populations of particles within embedded grid cells. Nevertheless, our approach suggests that such redistri-
bution should be applied to the genuine probability distributions f α instead of the lattice-based distributions g α , and that
Eq. (19) should be proﬁtably integrated in the scheme to account for (possible) subgrid-scale turbulent dynamics.
    Finally, by combining the simplicity and physical relevance of the shear-improved Smagorinsky model and the computa-
tional eﬃciency of the lattice Boltzmann method, here extended to handle composite multi-resolution grids, our numerical
scheme is expected to provide an asset in terms of manageability, accuracy and computational cost for future large-eddy
simulations of complex turbulent ﬂows.
