Abstract
The Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM), a weakly compressible approach, has attracted
considerable attention for turbulent flow simulations but remains limited in high-Reyn-
olds-number applications due to its reliance on uniform meshes. This study investigates
the interpolation-based Lattice Boltzmann Method (IBLBM) for direct numerical simula-
tion (DNS) of turbulent channel and duct flows using nonuniform grids. Simulations are
conducted for turbulent Poiseuille channel flows at Reτ = 180, 395, 640, and 1000,
as well as for turbulent duct flow at Reτ = 360. The predicted mean velocity profiles,
Reynolds stresses, vorticity distributions, and turbulent kinetic energy budgets show excel-
lent agreement with benchmark DNS data. The IBLBM scheme accurately resolves near-
wall turbulence and secondary flow structures in ducts while maintaining similar com-
putational overhead across different interpolation orders. For the 7- and 200-million-grid
cases at Reτ = 180 and 640, LBM with ∆+ ∼ 3 is approximately 1.9 and 3.7 times
faster, respectively, than sixth-order IBLBM; however, to match IBLBMs wall resolution
(∆+ ∼ 0.3) using ∆+ ∼ 1, LBM becomes four to six times slower. These results dem-
onstrate the robustness and accuracy of IBLBM for high-Reynolds-number wall-bounded
turbulence, offering an optimal balance between computational efficiency and physical
fidelity through the use of nonuniform grids.

1 Introduction

The Lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) (Aidun and Clausen 2010) has gained prominence as
a noteworthy alternative to Navier-Stokes solvers over the past three decades. The explicit
formulation of LBM makes it particularly well suited for parallel computations, and its
ease of implementation further enhances its appeal. These characteristics make LBM highly
efficient in addressing a wide range of flow problems, such as pressure-driven flows (Lin et

al. 2020; Wang et al. 2024), flows involving natural convection (Dubois et al. 2016), two-
phase flows (Huang et al. 2018), and porous media flows (Kuwata et al. 2020). Despite its
advantages, the original LBM is limited by its dependence on uniform Cartesian meshes,
which poses a notable constraint when simulating complex flows. This restriction becomes
particularly evident in wall-bounded turbulent flows, where the accurate resolution of the
near-wall region necessitates the use of fine meshes (Hsu et al. 2012).
    To overcome the constraints imposed by the necessity of uniform meshes, various inves-
tigations have used multiblock grid refinement techniques (Filippova and Hänel 1998; Wu
et al. 2020). These methodologies facilitate adaptable adjustment of grid resolution to cater
to the specific requirements of the flow field. Despite yielding favorable outcomes, these
approaches maintain the use of uniform meshes within individual blocks. As a consequence,
the handling of interfaces between distinct blocks remains a nontrivial challenge.
    Various alternative approaches have been explored to mitigate the limitations associated
with uniform mesh requirements. One such strategy involves the adoption of nonuniform
meshes, where the coupling between the discretization of momentum and physical space
is relaxed (He et al. 1996; He and Doolen 1997). Despite the localization of collisions, the
differential mesh width impedes the streaming operation from reaching adjacent grid nodes.
Consequently, interpolation techniques are used to recover the distribution function within
the computational mesh. The selection of an interpolation stencil significantly influences
the accuracy of predictions, with He et al. (1996) demonstrating that quadratic interpolation
achieves better accuracy, albeit in simulations conducted at low Reynolds laminar numbers.
    Furthermore, Cheng and Hung (2004) employed an upward-based Lagrangian interpola-
tion technique, whereas Krämer et al. (2017) introduced the concept of the Semi-Lagrangian
Lattice Boltzmann Method (SLLBM). In SLLBM, the precision of the reconstruction of the
distribution function is enhanced through the integration of the finite element method, albeit
at the cost of requiring a substantial amount of computational memory. In particular, Di Ilio
et al. (2018) conducted simulations of turbulent flow past a circular cylinder at a Reynolds
number of Re = 3900 using second-order SLLBM on body-fit meshes. These nonuniform
mesh approaches offer promising solutions to simulate complex flows, providing greater
flexibility and accuracy compared to traditional uniform mesh methodologies.
    The aforementioned methodologies are mainly based on the Lagrangian formulation. An
alternative avenue involves employing the Eulerian form of the discrete-lattice Boltzmann
equation (LBE). In this paradigm, the LBE can be addressed analogously to the convection
equation, utilizing the finite volume method (Nannelli and Succi 1992; Peng et al. 1998;
1999) or the finite difference method (Lee and Lin 2003).
    In this Eulerian approach, the collision term in the LBE is treated as a source term, and
the precision of both the time derivative and the advection term discretizations directly
influences the temporal and spatial accuracy of the solution. Additionally, the finite-volume
or finite-difference methods are employed to discretize the governing equations, facilitating
fluid flow simulation from an Eulerian perspective. These techniques offer an alternative
route for lattice Boltzmann simulations, presenting distinct trade-offs in terms of accuracy,
stability, and computational efficiency.
    Pressure-driven turbulent channel flows (Moser et al. 1999; Abe et al. 2001; Lee and
Moser 2015) have served as benchmark cases to assess the validity of direct numerical sim-
ulation studies of the Lattice Boltzmann method (LBM-DNS) (Suga et al. 2015; Gehrke et
al. 2017; Lee et al. 2018). The predominant focus of existing LBM-DNS investigations lies

in the realm of low Reynolds numbers, typically around Reτ ∼ 180 (Rebulk ∼ 5660) (Suga
et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2018).
    Nathen et al. (2018) conducted LBM-DNS at lower resolutions for channel flows at
Reτ =590. The uniform grid resolution is approximately ∆+ ∼ 6.48, leading to a 20%

number achieved in LBM-DNS is Reτ = 640 (Rebulk = 24000) (Wu et al. 2020), achieved
through successive block mesh refinement strategies with a grid count of 235 × 106 using
∆+ min ∼ 1, albeit with minor discontinuities at the block mesh interface. Recently, Xiang
et al. (2023) conducted turbulent duct flow up to Reτ = 2000 based on duct height using
D3Q19 SRT LBM and uniform mesh with a grid resolution of approximately ∆+ ∼ 3.4.
However, Lee et al. (2018) and Kand and Hassan (2013) indicated that the D3Q19 lattice
model is unsuitable for turbulent duct flow simulations, as the model generates additional
corner vortex. The anisotropy and associated defects of the D3Q19 model have also been
observed in jet flow simulations, as noted by Geller et al. (2013).
    As indicated by Smits and Marusic (2013), industrial applications demand Reynolds
numbers exceeding Reτ ≳ 1000 (Rebulk ∼ 40,000), thereby necessitating a thorough
investigation of the proposed scheme. Accordingly, this study employs direct numerical
simulation (DNS) of channel flows up to Reτ ∼ 1000 to evaluate the method’s capability
and performance under industrially relevant conditions. The selected grid density aligns
with that used in Navier–Stokes equation-based simulations (Moser et al. 1999; Abe et al.
2001; Lee and Moser 2015), enabling a comparative assessment of the interpolation-based
lattice Boltzmann method (IBLBM) across different interpolation orders.
    To date, performance evaluations beyond second-order interpolation remain scarce,
particularly in the context of complex turbulent flows. The present interpolation strate-
gies address challenges arising from nonuniform mesh widths, which inhibit the stream-
ing process from naturally reaching adjacent grid nodes. Furthermore, the method readily
accommodates different collision models, such as the single-relaxation-time and multi-
relaxation-time lattice Boltzmann methods (LBM) (Aidun and Clausen 2010), due to the
inherently localized nature of the collision operation.
    The paper is organized into distinct sections to facilitate a comprehensive understanding
of the presented research. In Sect. 2, the formulation of the lattice Boltzmann method is
explained, accompanied by a detailed discussion of the interpolation strategies used. Sec-
tion 3 offers an extensive exposition on the results derived from the analysis of the laminar
Poiseuille flow and the turbulent Poiseuille flow. Finally, Sect. 4 succinctly summarizes the
key findings and conclusions drawn from the study.

2.2 The Interpolation-Based Lattice Boltzmann Method

During the streaming step, meticulous consideration must be given to the departure lat-
tice point, xdep , at x − ei δt due to potential misalignment with the computational mesh
point and lattice point, particularly in nonuniform grids, i.e., ∆x, ∆y, ∆z ̸= δx. To rectify
this misalignment, the present study employs an interpolation process directly within the
physical domain. This interpolation process is instrumental in determining the values of the
post-collision distribution function, denoted f + (xdep ), at the departure points. The rationale
behind adopting this approach is grounded in the assumption that, in the context of incom-
pressible flow, physical properties exhibit continuity throughout the entire physical domain,
as postulated by He et al. (1996).
   The value of the post-collision distribution at the departure point is the summation of the
value of each support point multiplied by its respective interpolation coefficients. The equa-
tion can be expressed as follows:

2.3 GPU Implementation

In this investigation, 3-dimensional (3D) flow-field simulations are performed on multi-
GPU clusters. The GPU implementation builds upon previous research efforts (Lee et al.
2018; Chang et al. 2013; Hong et al. 2015). To efficiently manage the computational work-
load, a one-dimensional domain decomposition strategy is employed, as illustrated in Fig. 2.
   Each subdomain is assigned to a dedicated GPU, and the decomposition is performed
along the streamwise direction (e.g., the x-axis), dividing the global domain into equally
sized slabs, shown as green and dark blue boxes in Fig. 2. Within each GPUs memory, the
allocated storage for distribution functions is slightly enlarged to include buffer regions that
support interpolation near subdomain boundaries, depicted as light blue boxes in Fig. 2.
The number of buffer layers depends on the interpolation order: fourth-order interpolation
requires two layers on each side of a subdomain, whereas sixth-order interpolation requires
three.

   Communication between neighboring GPUs is facilitated via GPUDirect Remote Direct
Memory Access (RDMA) (2025), which enables accelerated data transfers between GPUs,
especially when used in conjunction with CUDA-aware MPI for inter-node communication.
At each time step, the streaming distribution functions near the subdomain boundaries—
specifically, the 10 directions out of 19 in the D3Q19 lattice model that are relevant to the
one-dimensional domain decomposition—are exchanged between adjacent GPUs.
   To improve scalability and computational performance, the implementation overlaps
communication with computation, following the approach adopted by Hong et al. (2015),
effectively hiding the latency associated with data transfers. While data exchange occurs at
subdomain boundaries, interior grid points that do not require updated halo data are com-
puted concurrently.

3.2 Turbulent Poiseuille Channel Flows

The current numerical procedure is used to calculate turbulent channel flows bounded by
walls driven by pressure. Non-slip boundary conditions are applied in the y direction, while
periodic conditions are enforced in the x and z directions. A constant pressure driving force
is included in the streamwise direction to attain the target Reynolds numbers.
    In this particular setup of wall-bounded flow, grid refinement is intentionally focused
near the walls to resolve the thin viscous sublayer adequately. As a result, a nonuniform grid
is generated in the vertical direction utilizing a hyperbolic tangent function, as elaborated
in Eq. 11.
    Four Reynolds numbers are under investigation: Reτ = 180, 395, 640, and 1000, based
on the friction velocity and the half-height of the channel. The Reτ = 180 case has been
extensively studied, while Reτ = 1000 has not been explored using direct numerical simu-
lations with the lattice Boltzmann method. Table 2 provides a comprehensive overview of
the grid configurations and lattice dimensions used throughout the study.
    To ensure efficient acquisition of results through direct numerical simulation while main-
taining a reasonable mesh size, subsequent scenarios maintain a grid spacing of approxi-
mately ∆+ ∼ 5 in both the span and the streamwise directions. Furthermore, the vertical
resolution is kept within the approximate range of 0.3 to 5 (Abe et al. 2001). These grid
densities were considered sufficient for direct numerical simulation using the Navier-Stokes
equation.
    Figure 6 depicts the predicted streamwise velocity utilizing various interpolation orders
(IO: 2 to 8) at Reynolds numbers Reτ = 180 (IO: 2,4,6), 640 (IO: 6,8), and 1000 (IO:
6,8). At Reτ = 180, the 2nd order interpolation presents an excessively accelerated profile,
whereas the 4th and 6th order interpolations exhibit similar results, though the 6th order
results are marginally better. The impacts of these interpolation orders are further scruti-
nized by contrasting the turbulent vorticity distributions, as shown in Fig. 7. Similarly to
observations in streamwise velocity distributions at Reτ = 180, the 2nd order interpolation
deviates from the benchmark solutions. In contrast, the 4th and 6th order interpolations show
similar results, with the latter being marginally better.
    At a higher Reynolds number of Reτ = 640, we investigated the effects of interpolations
of the order 6th and 8th, as shown in Figs. 6 and 7, which reveal marginally different results.
This is also true for the Reτ = 1000 case, where marginal difference results are observed.
Consequently, for subsequent simulations, we adopt 6th-order interpolation for the Reynolds
number investigated.
    The performance of the current interpolation-based lattice Boltzmann method (IBLBM)
in simulating mean velocity profiles across various Reynolds numbers up to Reτ = 1000
is evident from Fig. 8, which demonstrates excellent alignment with benchmark solutions
using direct numerical simulation data of Moser et al. (1999); Abe et al. (2001, 2004), and
Lee and Moser (2015). In particular, the model accurately captures the heightened mean
velocities beyond the logarithmic region at high Reynolds numbers in the center of the
channel.

3.3 Turbulent Poiseuille Duct Flow

To further investigate the 6th order IBLBM applicability with two inhomogeneous direc-
tions, the method is applied to simulate turbulent Poiseuille flow through a square duct at
Reτ = 360. For duct flow, Reτ is defined based on the duct height H as Reτ = uτ H/ν .
    The computational domain of the conduit is defined as 2πH × H × H in the x, y, and z
directions. No-slip boundary conditions are applied at all four duct walls, while a periodic
boundary condition is imposed in the streamwise (x) direction. A nonuniform grid, gener-
ated using the same hyperbolic tangent function as in the turbulent Poiseuille channel flow,
is employed in the wall-normal directions (i.e., the y- and z-axes). Sixth-order interpolation
is used for both laminar and turbulent duct flow simulations.
    Previous studies by Kang and Hassan (2013) and Lee et al. (2018), both using a uniform
grid, indicated that the D3Q19 lattice model generates additional corner vortices in turbu-
lent duct flows. Furthermore, Lee et al. (2018) demonstrated that even in the laminar regime,
the D3Q19 model produces an artificial secondary flow pattern.
    Figure 12 illustrates the predicted streamwise velocity contours and secondary flow pat-
terns for laminar flow at Reτ = 30 using the D3Q19 and D3Q27 lattice models. The results
reveal that the D3Q19 model introduces spurious secondary flow, whereas the D3Q27
model, as expected, remains free of such effects. The magnitude of the secondary flow
predicted by the D3Q19 model is on the order of 10−4 relative to the streamwise velocity.
Despite employing a nonuniform grid (64 × 64), the findings align well with those of Lee
et al. (2018). Consequently, the D3Q27 model is chosen for simulating turbulent duct flow.
    Table 7 presents the grid configuration and lattice size employed for the turbulent duct
flow simulation. In the streamwise direction, the grid spacing is set to ∆+ ∼ 5, while ∆+ in
the wall-normal direction, is maintained within the range of 0.35 to 5.03 to ensure consis-
tency with direct numerical simulation results.

4 Conclusion

This study presents numerical simulations of turbulent channel and duct flows using the
interpolation-based Lattice Boltzmann Method (IBLBM) with nonuniform meshes. The
investigation begins with laminar Poiseuille flow featuring wall injection and suction, where
analytical solutions are used as a benchmark to assess the effect of interpolation order. In this
configuration, the maximum velocity shifts toward the suction boundary, producing a more
pronounced near-wall velocity gradient that resembles turbulent boundary layers—condi-
tions under which nonuniform meshes offer distinct advantages. Results show that IBLBM
with fourth-order interpolation and higher achieves accuracy comparable to conventional
uniform-mesh LBM while requiring fewer grid points, whereas second-order interpolation
yields reduced predictive accuracy.

    The method was further applied to turbulent duct flow with two inhomogeneous direc-

ing spurious secondary flows. Predicted mean velocity contours and secondary flow patterns
agreed well with reference data, validating the approach for more complex geometries.
    Performance analysis shows that, for the same total grid count, the computational over-
head of IBLBM remains of the same order across interpolation schemes. For the 7- and
200-million-grid cases at Reτ = 180 and 640, LBM with ∆+ ∼ 3 is approximately 1.9 and
3.7 times faster, respectively, than sixth-order IBLBM. However, to match the wall resolu-
tion of IBLBM (∆+ ∼ 0.3), adopting ∆+ ∼ 1, LBM becomes four to six times slower,
substantially increasing computational cost and negating its performance advantage.
    Overall, the results demonstrate that IBLBM with nonuniform meshes is a robust and
accurate approach for simulating wall-bounded turbulent flows. By enabling high near-wall
resolution with fewer grid points, it achieves an optimal balance between computational
efficiency and accuracy, making it particularly effective for high-Reynolds-number simula-
tions where wall resolution is critical.
