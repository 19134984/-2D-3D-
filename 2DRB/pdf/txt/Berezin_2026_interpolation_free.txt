1. Introduction

    The Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) [1] is one of the most popular numerical methods for computational ﬂuid dynamics (CFD).
Its most important advantages are the simplicity of the numerical expressions for the scheme and locality. As a consequence, it is
convenient to implement and parallelize on any modern computer, and, therefore, many high-performance implementations exist [2–
5], and are used for relevant ﬂuid dynamics problems [6–9]. LBM has been greatly expanded by many authors to extend its area of
applicability [10–13].
    The need for grid reﬁnement is well established in CFD methods [14]. The mesh resolution is chosen as a trade-oﬀ between the
ability to resolve ﬁne ﬂow structures and the computational complexity. It is often advantageous to assign high-resolution meshes to
some regions, and to coarsen the mesh in the others to save on the amount of computation and data size. At the interface between
the grids with diﬀerent resolutions, a method of grid pairing should be implemented. The development of one such method is the
topic for the current study.
    The method applied in the interface region should not alter the physics described in the simulation, and the amount of spuri-
ous noise [15] and ﬂow perturbations generated by information transfer between grids should be minimized. It turns out that the
properties of the LBM method (mass conservation, the second order of accuracy, locality, simplicity) are diﬃcult to preserve.
    There are two main directions in the development of mesh reﬁnement methods for LBM [16,17]: volume-based (also known as
cell-centered) and node-based (also known as pointwise, or vertex-centered). In the node-based methods, in the reﬁnement, an LBM
node is inserted between the existing mesh nodes. In the volume-based methods, the mesh is interpreted as a collection of cells, alike
CFD ﬁnite volume methods, and each cell is subdivided.

    In LBM, there is the streaming step, in which the variables (populations 𝑓𝑖 , 𝑖 ∈ {0, … , 𝑚 − 1}) are copied from node to node in
the corresponding direction, and the local collision step. The set of directions for streaming is called the LBM stencil. The com-
monly used local collision operators that are mentioned hereafter are Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK), two relaxation time (TRT), and
multirelaxation time (MRT) [1].
    If the grid is not uniform, the streaming into the nodes on the reﬁnement interface is performed from the places where no mesh
node is present. It is a usual practice to interpolate data into those points.
    In the original work by Fillipova [18], the reﬁnement is node-based and second-order interpolation in time and space is used.
Later works have shown that the second order is not enough and a discontinuity in the pressure across the interface is present [19].
Higher-order interpolation is used in more recent works on node-based methods [20,21].
    An important step in the node-based method is the rescaling of the non-equilibrium parts of 𝑓𝑖 during the LBM step on the
interface. The expression for rescaling depends on the collision operator. For any new advanced LBM collision operator, the rescaling
expression should be derived before the node-based reﬁnement can be used. Fillipova has introduced the method for the BGK collision
operator [18]. An alternative expression for pre-collision 𝑓𝑖 was proposed in [22]. Rescaling for MRT collision is used in [20,21], for
hybrid recursive regularization in [16], and for entropic LBM in [23]. In [24], the rescaling is performed for the non-equilibrium part
of the moments.
    In some methods, instead of interpolating 𝑓𝑖 , its moments are interpolated, and 𝑓𝑖 are reconstructed from the moments [25]. Such
methods can result in higher-order interpolation realized with more locality [26–28].
    The interpolation in time is also required if the time step is split together with the grid step. In the previous methods, interpolation
in time was avoided by enlarging the buﬀer zone between the regions [29]. In [30], the technique is introduced by diﬀerentiating
between initial value problem and boundary value problem for ﬁnding the information outside a region with ﬁner mesh. It was found
that, in 1D, the coarse streaming step can pass through the ﬁne nodes. That way, the information in the ﬁner nodes is found by
streaming and without interpolation in time. As a result, the solution error was reduced.
    The volume-based reﬁnement is commonly described by the two operations: explosion and coalescing [17,31]. In the explosion,
the mesh cell is subdivided into several cells, and 𝑓𝑖 are spread between the resulting cells. Although the authors state that the method
does not contain any interpolation, this statement should be understood in terms of computational complexity, i.e., the expression
for the interpolated values is not a polynomial that depends on the coordinates, and fewer arithmetic operations are needed. This
may be the reason why the volume-based reﬁnement is common in the high-performance solutions [4]. In fact, the explosion step is
a low-order interpolation in space. Higher-order methods were proposed in [32–34]. In the coalescing, several cells of the ﬁne mesh
are merged into one cell on the coarser grid. This operation can be interpreted as ﬁltering [16]. Filtering is manually introduced in
node-based methods to reduce ﬂuctuations which are not resolved by the coarse grid [19,35].
    The volume-based method performs correctly without population rescaling [17], and this opens more ﬂexibility to the choice of the
collision operator. Moreover, this volume-based method is exactly mass-conservative by construction. On the other hand, the method
can’t be easily applied to stencils with increased number of velocities, such as D3Q39 which is used in state-of-the-art aerodynamic
LBM solutions [10].
    In total, the interpolation on the mesh interface leads to the loss of some LBM advantages. In LBM, the streaming operation is
an exact copy, which is why the method has no dissipation, while interpolation is a dissipative step. Interpolation with a suﬃcient
order requires wider stencils, increases computational complexity, and makes it harder to parallelize the simulation. Furthermore,
interpolation leads to mass loss and may introduce lattice anisotropy which is not present in LBM on a uniform grid.
    Instead of performing the interpolation, LBM streaming can be replaced by a ﬁnite diﬀerence [36] or ﬁnite element [37] scheme.
In these cases, the same disadvantages are present.
    Therefore, as the main goal of this work, we desire a pure LBM grid reﬁnement. In [38], we introduced the method which allows
streaming populations from node to node directly on the mesh reﬁnement interface, and thus, the interpolation step is not required,
and the scheme for every node is an LBM scheme. The method requires the introduction of special stencils on the boundary, which
connect mesh nodes between the coarse and ﬁne grids. The special stencils contain a diﬀerent number of discrete velocities. To
formulate the connection between the special stencils and the stencils of the uniform portions of the grid, the moment matching
condition from [39] was used.
    In [39], the moment matching condition was introduced as the gateway to the Particles-on-Demand (PonD) method for the purpose
of simulation of the ﬂows with arbitrary Mach number. The ability to simulate ﬂows with Mach number over 100 was proven [39],
while some beneﬁcial properties of LBM were lost. The method is supplemented by interpolation which was borrowed from the
semi-Langrangian LBM [40–42]. The populations recalibration from one stencil to another is performed by a computationally heavy
step which involves matrix inversion and multiplication. An alternate version of recalibration was introduced in [43] which is based
on the regularization in the representation of 𝑓𝑖 [44]. Despite the use of interpolation, it was shown that the method can be made
mass conservative [45]. Conservation is also obtainable with the use of the ﬁnite volume method instead of usual streaming [46].
The use of moment matching to develop LBM on stretched meshes without altering the mesh topology is found in [47].
    The recalibration of populations can serve to shift the frame of reference in the representation of the distribution function [48–50],
in order to stabilize the collision by adjusting the lattice temperature [12]. Continuing from [38,51,52], we use recalibration of
populations between stencils for interpolation-free mesh transitions. In comparison to what was reported before, we found more
stencils for grid transition, improved the recalibration method, and introduce the new mesh transition scheme for the reﬁnement
interface. Furthermore, the method is now applicable for the two-relaxation-times (TRT) collision operator. The preliminary results
of the current work were reported in [53].

    The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we remind the basics of the well-known LBM method, and introduce extended

2. LBM with the TRT collision operator

   We start with a detailed overview of the standard LBM in order to introduce new notations that are convenient for this work.
Unlike the standard LBM, we mainly use a set of streaming vectors rather than a set of discrete LBM velocities. To unambiguously
deﬁne streaming by these vectors, we introduce an extended LBM stencil notation, as well as a graphical notation to describe the
operation of a numerical scheme at integer and half-integer times.
   This work uses the TRT collision operator, which is easy to implement and has an additional free parameter to control the
accuracy and stability of calculations. But more importantly, the TRT operator ﬁts very naturally with the recalibration procedure of
LBM populations, which we introduce further.

streaming vectors 𝐫𝑖 = Δ𝑡 ξ0 𝐯𝑖 in general include
 • the time step Δ𝑡,
 • the parameter ξ0 ,
 • the quadrature points 𝐯 .
                          𝑖

For simulations on a uniform lattice, the parameters Δ𝑡 and ξ0 are ﬁxed for the entire simulation domain and can be speciﬁed only once.

to diﬀerent time steps. To distinguish between such stencils, in this work, the values Δ𝑡 and ξ20 are added in parentheses: D𝑛Q𝑚(Δ𝑡, ξ20 ).

    To complete the description of the extended stencil notation D𝑛Q𝑚(Δ𝑡, ξ20 ), let us clarify the meaning of each parameter in the
notation. The quadrature, denoted by D𝑛Q𝑚, deﬁnes points 𝐯𝑖 without relation to spatial or temporal scale. The time step Δ𝑡 deﬁnes
the temporal scale of the stencil. Δ𝑡 is the time span of the streaming step by the corresponding stencil. Finally, for a given quadrature
and a time step, through the relation 𝐫𝑖 = Δ𝑡 ξ0 𝐯𝑖 , the parameter ξ0 deﬁnes the spatial scale of the stencil. Let us also note that ξ0 can
be considered as the speed of sound or the square root of the lattice temperature. To summarize, the quadrature (D𝑛Q𝑚) deﬁnes the
shape of the stencil, and parameters Δ𝑡 and ξ0 deﬁne its temporal and spatial scales, respectively.

3. Populations recalibration

    The incomplete overlapping of the streaming vectors with nodes near the boundary of the non-uniform lattice is not the only issue
of node-based multiscale LBM schemes. Following the pull paradigm of the streaming step, some nodes also request populations from
the nodes where the set of populations corresponds to a diﬀerent stencil. So, even if a population is requested from an existing node,
it may not be explicitly present there.
    The population sets of mismatched stencils diﬀer because they are solutions of diﬀerent systems of equations (9). This is obvious
for stencils of diﬀerent shapes (diﬀerent quadratures): not only do the directions of the streaming vectors diﬀer, but also their number
and, consequently, the number of equations and variables. For stencils of diﬀerent scales, the statement also holds true. If the stencils
diﬀer only in spatial scale (in parameter ξ0 ), then their streaming vectors have diﬀerent lengths, changing the original system of
equations. If the Δ𝑡 ξ0 product remains constant, so that the streaming vectors do not change, then the conservation of viscosity (13)
necessitates the change of the parameter 𝜔+ and the corresponding change in the collision operator in its entirety.
    To freely switch between sets of populations corresponding to diﬀerent stencils, an appropriate procedure should be developed. In
this section, we present a general method for recalibration of populations — a local in time and space transition between populations
sets of arbitrary stencils. Some recalibration methods are known from previous work on multiscale LBM [18,38,57]. However, they
are applicable only to a limited set of stencils and, as we demonstrate later, do not allow to preserve the second-order accuracy of
standard LBM.
    Below we use the tensor notation. The indices 𝑎, 𝑏, and 𝑑 denote Cartesian components; 𝑖 and 𝑗 are used to index the discrete
velocity space. For example, 𝑐𝑖𝑎 is the 𝑎th Cartesian component of the 𝑖th discrete velocity of an LBM stencil. Einstein’s summation
convention is used for all indices except the index 𝑖.

3.1. Stencils of the same shape

   We start with the special case of recalibration between populations sets which correspond to stencils of the same shape: {𝑓𝑖 } of St1 =
D𝑛Q𝑚(Δ𝑡1 , ξ20, 1 ) and {𝑔𝑖 } of St2 = D𝑛Q𝑚(Δ𝑡2 , ξ20, 2 ). The stencils have the same quadrature points, but diﬀerent scaling parameters.
Our method for recalibration between such stencils follows the ideas of the original work by Fillipova [18], and, technically, it is the
scaling of non-equilibrium populations. However, the results of this section outperform previous similar approaches in both accuracy
and applicability.

3.1.3. Intermediate discussion
    In this section, we, for the ﬁrst time, derived the recalibration formulas for transition between stencils with the same quadrature,
that are valid up to the second order in ﬂow velocity 𝑢. Also, for the ﬁrst time, the recalibration formulas are applicable to the LBM
with the TRT collision operator.
    Our results are readily consistent with the earlier recalibration methods [18,38,57]. To obtain the preceding recalibrations from
our formulas, it is necessary to neglect the terms quadratic in 𝑢. Under this approximation Ξ𝑖 = 0, and the non-equilibrium populations
(both pre- and post-collision) corresponding to counter-directional velocities are equal. Thus, only the relations (21a), (28a) remain
non-trivial, and they simplify to a single-index form. In this case, and with the reduction of TRT to BGK (𝜔+ = 𝜔− = 1∕𝜏), (28a)
reproduces the recalibration in [18], and (21a) reproduces the recalibration in [57]. Besides, we note that in the indicated works
only the recalibration between stencils with diﬀerent Δ𝑡, but the same ξ0 was considered.
    Why are 𝑢2 terms so important? The most reliable evidence is present in the testing section, but some considerations can be
made already. Such terms sustain the natural precision of LBM. In particular, they are present in equilibrium populations (8) from
the beginning. Also, Chapman–Enskog analysis Appendix A shows that their consideration is necessary to correctly reproduce the
Navier–Stokes equation (A.28). Finally, taking the quadratic terms into account can be especially important when linear terms are
absent. For example, in the simple case of a two-dimensional Poiseuille ﬂow 𝐮 = (0, 𝑢(𝑥)) and for the D2Q9 quadrature, Γ𝑖 = 0 for all
indices except those corresponding to the diagonal velocities 𝐜𝑖 ∼ (±1, ±1). That is, for ﬁve of the nine populations, the recalibration
is incorrect if the Ξ𝑖 term is neglected.
    Let us discuss some properties of the obtained recalibration formulas. Although {𝑓𝑖 } and {𝑔𝑖 } are solutions of diﬀerent systems of
equations, they must reproduce the same solution of the Navier–Stokes equation. So, the macroscopic parameters of the ﬂow should
not change under local recalibration. The constancy of viscosity is controlled by the choice of 𝜔+ , which is determined uniquely
from (13) for given Δ𝑡, ξ0 and 𝜈. The conservation of moments 𝜌 and 𝜌𝐮 is guaranteed by equalities

3.3. General recalibration discussion

   Above we described two methods for population recalibration: the ﬁrst one works for stencils of the same shape, the second one
works for stencils of the same scale. By using them together, one can freely switch between arbitrary LBM stencils. Such a general
recalibration has a two-stage form. To switch from D𝑛Q𝑚1 (Δ𝑡1 , ξ20, 1 ) to D𝑛Q𝑚2 (Δ𝑡2 , ξ20, 2 ), one of the following ways can be used:

        D𝑛Q𝑚1 (Δ𝑡1 , ξ20, 1 ) → D𝑛Q𝑚1 (Δ𝑡2 , ξ20, 2 ) → D𝑛Q𝑚2 (Δ𝑡2 , ξ20, 2 )                                                               (42)

or

        D𝑛Q𝑚1 (Δ𝑡1 , ξ20, 1 ) → D𝑛Q𝑚2 (Δ𝑡1 , ξ20, 1 ) → D𝑛Q𝑚2 (Δ𝑡2 , ξ20, 2 ),                                                              (43)

where, in the ﬁrst case, the scale recalibration (Section 3.1) is followed by the shape recalibration (Section 3.2), and the other way
around in the second case. These two recalibration paths are equivalent.
    In the process of the two-stage recalibration, a “phantom” intermediate stencil appears, which participates in recalibration only
formally. Below such stencils are not speciﬁed explicitly.
    The two-stage recalibration method has already been proposed in our previous work [38]. In this work, we signiﬁcantly improved
the scale recalibration by taking into account the terms quadratic in the ﬂow velocity 𝑢. We showed in the tests (Section 5) that such
terms are critically important to maintain the original second-order accuracy of the standard LBM. The method is now also extended
to LBM with the TRT collision operator. TRT ﬁts very well with improved populations recalibration, since both cases involve a pair
of populations corresponding to counter-directional discrete velocities.
    Let us discuss the accuracy of the obtained recalibration formulas. The scale recalibration now takes into account terms up to 𝑢2
in the ﬁrst order of the Knudsen number expansion. While taking the cubic terms in ﬂow velocity into account exceeds the accuracy
of the standard LBM, the terms corresponding to 𝜖 2 can in principle be considered. However, based on (A.8), (A.11), (A.13), (A.21),
and (A.22), such terms contain second derivatives of conserved moments or products of two ﬁrst-order derivatives. As a result, they
are suppressed by the operator Δ𝑡 ξ0 𝜕𝑎 that corresponds to the small ratio |⟨𝐫𝑖 ⟩|∕𝑙, where |⟨𝐫𝑖 ⟩| is the average length of the streaming
vectors, and 𝑙 is the typical system spatial scale.
    Scale recalibration in theory allows one to get an arbitrarily small or arbitrarily large stencil. The only reasonable limitations for
the target stencil are the same as in the standard LBM: Δ𝑡 ≪ 𝑡0 , where 𝑡0 is the typical system temporal scale, and |⟨𝐫𝑖 ⟩| ≪ 𝑙.
    As for the moment matching, the only approximation here is the incorrect accounting of a small number of higher-order moments.
But even in the worst case considered in this work, this involves only three of the thirteen required moments (see Table B.2).
    Despite the indicated approximations, we believe that the presented recalibration method works extremely well, at the same time
maintaining a relatively simple structure. The recalibration method can be applied in a wide range of LBM areas: for formulating
highly accurate boundary conditions, for schemes with adaptive collision operators, etc. In particular, expressions (19) and (26)
can be considered as improved initial conditions for pre-collision and post-collision populations, respectively. Next, we apply the
population recalibration method to construct an interpolation-free LBM scheme on non-uniform lattice.

4.1.3. Discussion of the CT and the NCT approaches
    CT and NCT schemes are just two of the many possible schemes provided by our method. The CT scheme has two advantages
over the NCT scheme. First, in CT scheme all stencils correspond to the same speed of sound, it is a more promising candidate for
acoustic problems. Second, special stencils of this scheme allow to reﬁne the lattice in an arbitrary subdomain of the initial coarse
lattice, which is very useful for adaptive mesh reﬁnement.
    The extension of the considered schemes to the corners of the reﬁnement boundary does not involve any additional complications.
The stencils distributions of CT and NCT schemes for such cases are shown in Figs. 5 and 6, respectively.
    Let us again summarize basic principles of the schemes construction in our method. Each node uses only those stencils whose
streaming vectors are mapped onto other nodes exactly. To fulﬁll this condition for the border nodes (ﬂag 3) we add the additional
transition nodes (ﬂag 4) that use special stencils. The stencils are used to obtain population sets in the internodal space of a purely
coarse lattice. In other words, special stencils in Fig. 3 allow one to create a purely ﬁne lattice from a purely coarse one. If a node
receives a population request which originates from a diﬀerent stencil, its populations set is locally recalibrated to the target stencil
to obtain the required population. As a result, each node at each required time instant receives a complete set of populations of its
stencil and is able to perform a collision step.
    In the standard LBM on uniform lattices, after the collision, all populations in the interior of the domain are streamed to some
neighbors, and this fact ensures the exact conservation of mass and momentum. In our reﬁnement approach, nodes 2, 3, 4 have very
few neighbors of the same type, if any at all. Therefore, they play the role of boundary nodes for the coarse and ﬁne regions. The

4.2. Description of the algorithm

    Here we provide algorithms for our particular implementation of CT and NCT schemes.
    A non-uniform lattice is constructed with the ZAMR tool [58] of the aiwlib software [59]. The package provides convenient mesh
generation, access to neighbors by the position vectors, and cost-eﬃcient storage of data associated with the nodes of a non-uniform
lattice.
    The main element of the code is the data structure for an LBM node (Algorithm 1). Each node contains two sets of data arrays
for the pre-collision (line 4) and post-collision (line 5) populations, an integer ﬂag (line 2) that is used to determine how the node
is processed in the algorithm, and an index to its stencil (line 3). We also include the set of nodes from which the populations are
requested during the streaming step (line 6). This set is determined by the streaming vectors 𝐫𝑖 of the node’s stencil. The nodes in the
set are called the neighbors of the original node.
    For the node structure, several methods are proposed. Streaming (line 7) and collision (line 21) are standard LBM steps for uniform
lattice regions. Streaming_with_recalibration (line 15) is called when the stencils of some neighboring nodes diﬀer from the stencil of
the described node. In these cases, a local recalibration (line 12) of the post-collision populations set performs.
    The last three methods — save (line 26), restore (line 27), and recalibrate_to_stencil (line 28) — are only needed for border nodes
with ﬂag 3. There are two issues in the processing of these nodes. First, only the border nodes are processed at integer and half-
integer times by diﬀerent stencils, which results in two diﬀerent sequential pairs of streaming and collision. The ﬁnal post-collision
populations of each operations pair need to be stored. Second, since stencil is a node variable, the initial and the target stencils
of the zero population in the streaming_with_recalibration method are always the same, and, therefore, this method does not provide
recalibration for it.

6. Conclusion

    We believe that the most impactful contribution of our work is the development of the solid theoretical foundation for construction
of interpolation-free lattice reﬁnement method for LBM with TRT collision operator. The method uses only the natural framework
of LBM schemes. Our construction retains such important LBM properties as stability and the second order of accuracy. This work
provides two main instruments that are used in the method construction: the recalibration procedure between diﬀerent LBM stencils
and special stencils that connect ﬁne and coarse lattices.
    The key ideas of the method have already been formulated in our previous article [38], but the current work contains a number
of important improvements, that, we hope, make our method relevant for practical use.
    We proposed the new recalibration procedure for LBM population sets of arbitrary stencils. As before, in general recalibration has
two stages: the rescaling of the non-equilibrium populations parts for stencils of diﬀerent scale and the moment matching for stencils
of diﬀerent shape. The ﬁrst stage, scale recalibration, is now valid up to quadratic terms in ﬂow velocity, that, as we have shown, is
necessary to preserve the second order of accuracy. Note that although the current work considers only 2D geometry, the method is
valid for 3D as well. As a result, the presented recalibration method allows free and high-precision switching between arbitrary LBM
stencils. We believe that the recalibration method itself is the important result of this work, and it can be applied in a wide variety
of LBM areas.
    The improved scale recalibration stage is natural for the use with the TRT collision operator. In the general case, it involves the
two populations corresponding to 𝑖 and 𝑖̄ indexes (even when TRT reduces to BGK). The interesting point is that while the moment
matching is valid for the LBM stencils corresponding to the same kinetic Boltzmann equation, the scaling procedure has no such
limitation. While moment matching equates the moments, the rescaling stage is based on equating the expressions which depend on
the ﬁrst derivatives of the conserved moments. Thus, in principle, we believe that it is possible to develop a procedure to completely
replace the moment matching step with derivative matching. One can expect that such a modiﬁcation will simplify the complete
recalibration process from both an ideological and a technical point of view.

                                                                     231
A. Berezin, A. Perepelkina and V. Levchenko                                                     Computers and Mathematics with Applications 212 (2026) 210–237


    Note that scale recalibration is also necessary for sets of populations corresponding to TRT collision operators that diﬀer only in
the parameter Λ. In the majority of our tests, we keep Λ the same for the entire simulation domain, but it is completely optional. One
can use such a freedom for precise tuning of accuracy and stability.
    Using the recalibration procedure, we developed method for building schemes on non-uniform lattices, which require special
stencils that connect lattice nodes of diﬀerent scales. Based on the method, we constructed and tested two schemes on two-dimensional
non-uniform lattices and also provided new, most local, special stencils for these schemes. The method is easily generalized onto many
dimensions, and works for any complex shapes of the reﬁnement boundary. It is only necessary to formulate the correct processing
of nodes near the boundary and ﬁnd suitable special stencils. Looking ahead, we can already build 3D schemes, but their precise
veriﬁcation is a subject for future work.
    We want to highlight that the two schemes presented in this work are just two samples from the variety of schemes that can be
made by our construction method. The NCT and CT schemes were originally constructed as a scheme with diﬀerent ξ0 stencils and
a scheme with the same ξ0 stencils, respectively. Together, these schemes involve all cases of populations recalibration in Section 3.
Although both of the above schemes demonstrate good results, for practical use we recommend the CT scheme. It has greater locality,
is more convenient for adaptive mesh reﬁnement, and gets better results in acoustic problems.
    On the basis of the proposed framework, even more practical developments of LBM schemes are possible. The method itself can
be used to perform linking of any conﬁgurations of nodes. In case of non-binary reﬁnement, unstructured lattices, or even meshless
LBM, it is always possible to construct a quadrature of a desired order by linking a node to its neighbors, and then use the proposed
recalibration method to enable streaming between diﬀerent stencils.
    Unlike the standard LBM, the mass and momentum conservation near the reﬁnement boundary is not exact. Nevertheless, we
believe that the proposed method can be expanded to resolve this. The solution may become clearer after formulating the scheme in
the push paradigm of the streaming step.
    We expect further developments of the proposed framework to improve accuracy and eﬃciency of future LBM simulations.
